% introduction.tex -- Introduction

The protein folding problem, predicting three-dimensional structure from amino acid sequence, stood as one of the grand challenges in molecular biology for half a century. AlphaFold2~\cite{jumperHighlyAccurateProtein2021} and RoseTTAFold~\cite{baekRoseTTAFold2021} effectively solved it, achieving experimental-level accuracy across the proteome and earning the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. With structure prediction largely in hand, the frontier has shifted decisively toward the inverse problem: \emph{designing} novel protein sequences with desired properties~\cite{kuhlmanBradleyAdvances2019,huangComingAgeDeNovo2016}. This shift has catalyzed a wave of deep generative models for protein design, from structure-conditioned methods like ProteinMPNN~\cite{dauparasProteinMPNN2022} and RFdiffusion~\cite{watsonRFdiffusion2023} to sequence-level approaches including protein language models~\cite{madaniProGenLanguageModeling2023,ferruzProtGPT2DeepUnsupervised2022}, MSA-conditioned transformers~\cite{raoMSATransformer2021}, and diffusion models~\cite{alamdariProteinGeneration2023}.

These methods are powerful, but they share a common requirement: large training corpora. Structure-conditioned models need backbone templates; sequence-level models are typically pretrained on millions of sequences from UniRef or similar databases and then fine-tuned or conditioned on family-specific alignments. This creates a blind spot. The median Pfam family contains fewer than 100 full-length members~\cite{mistryPfamProteinFamilies2021}, and the long tail of protein space (orphan families, newly discovered clades, engineered libraries) has even less data. In this scarce-data regime, learned generative models overfit, collapse in diversity, or fail to train altogether. Yet these small families are precisely where generative tools could have the greatest impact, by expanding sparse natural repertoires for functional characterization or directed evolution~\cite{wuMachineLearningAidedProtein2019}.

We propose a fundamentally different approach that requires no training at all. We observe that the modern Hopfield energy~\cite{ramsauerHopfieldNetworksAll2021} over a set of stored protein sequences defines a Boltzmann distribution whose score function (the gradient of the log-density) is given in closed form by the residual of a single attention operation. Langevin dynamics on this energy yields a training-free sampler that we call \emph{stochastic attention}: each iteration performs a softmax-weighted pull toward stored family members, a contraction, and an isotropic Gaussian perturbation, requiring only two matrix-vector products per step ($\mathcal{O}(dK)$ cost, no GPU, no backpropagation).

The key question this paper addresses is: \emph{what can you generate from a small protein family alignment with no training?} Across seven Pfam families spanning a ten-fold range of family sizes ($K{=}37$--$420$) and a seven-fold range of sequence lengths ($L{=}23$--$262$), we show that stochastic attention produces novel sequences that preserve family-level amino acid composition (KL${}<0.06$) and achieve substantial novelty ($0.42$--$0.65$ in PCA space), while folding into the correct three-dimensional architecture as confirmed by ESMFold~\cite{linEvolutionaryScaleModeling2023} structure prediction and TM-align~\cite{zhangTMalign2005} structural comparison. We characterize the critical inverse temperature $\beta^*$ at which the sampler transitions from diffuse exploration to pattern retrieval, and show that $\beta^*$ can be predicted from PCA dimensionality alone ($R^2{=}0.97$), enabling fully automatic operation from a seed alignment. A systematic scaling study reveals stable generation quality down to $K{=}20$ sequences. Head-to-head comparison with profile HMMs~\cite{hmmer3}, EvoDiff~\cite{alamdariProteinGeneration2023}, and the MSA Transformer~\cite{raoMSATransformer2021} reveals that stochastic attention uniquely balances compositional fidelity, sequence novelty, and structural viability without external training data.
